Sixgree maps your LinkedIn network out to six degrees, so every piece of its look tries to say one of two things: *degrees* and *tiers*. This is how each choice was made, including the ones we threw away.

## The icon: an S that is also a 6

The first icon was a placeholder: you as a white dot in the middle, with your circles round you in the tier colours. It explained the app, but it looked like every network app.

The next one drew a 6 in a single gold line around a glowing core, ending in a small ring, a degree sign. Closer, but busy at small sizes.

The icon we kept, called F1 while we compared a dozen candidates, is one tapered gold curve that draws an S. A round gold dot sits in its lower bowl, so the S also reads as a 6, and the dot is you. A small ring at the top closes it: six degrees. It sits on a deep navy square. The app icon adds faint tier rings and a glow behind the dot; the favicon drops them, because at 16 pixels the plain version reads better.

## The wordmark: dots that become a degree

"Sixgree" is set in Manrope ExtraBold, shipped inside the app so it never loads from anywhere. The dot over the i is gold, the S tier. The early versions stacked the tier dots upward or faded them out. The one that won, a family vote, runs the dots in a level line to the right of the i, across the whole word: gold, purple, blue, green, grey. The last one isn't a dot but a gold ring, the same size and height as the i's dot, so the name ends on a degree sign: Sixgree°.

On white the word is ink and the colour lives in the dots. On a dark look the word is gold.

## The tiers: a rarity ladder

Tiers run S, A, B, C, D, and their colours borrow the ladder people already know from games: gold, purple, blue, green, grey. C used to be a muddy colour that sat too close to D; making it green turned the five into a ladder you can read at a glance, on the map and in the wordmark.

## Glass, and fewer choices

The app used to offer six button styles and eight looks. Most people never changed them, and each one was another thing to keep readable. Now buttons are frosted glass by default, with Soft as the one alternative, and two looks went. Daylight, the light look, became macOS-style glass: translucent white panels with a strong blur, a hairline edge, a soft shadow, and darker greys than Apple's own so small text stays readable on white.

The page tabs had a colour each and a gradient on one of them. None of it meant anything, so they became one calm style: the tab you're on is a solid pill, the rest are plain.

## The website

The site is light only, with soft white download keys carrying the Apple and Microsoft marks. The header carries the icon and wordmark large enough to read, and each page link is a quiet grey pill; the page you're on fills with a tier colour, in the wordmark's order.

## What stays fixed

Every choice above can change again except two: the colours always mean tiers, and the rings always mean degrees. If a design makes either of those harder to read, it loses.
